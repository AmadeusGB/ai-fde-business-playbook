# How to use this book

This book is written for business owners and technical teams. Business owners can use it to select artificial intelligence (AI) projects, allocate resources and responsibilities, and verify business outcomes. Technical teams can use it to translate business objectives into requirements for workflows, permissions, evaluation, and human takeover. Business, finance, and procurement staff involved in a project can also use the book to check delivery commitments.

The book frequently refers to forward deployed engineers (FDEs). These engineers work directly with business users and help build, modify, and deliver systems. Their job is to bring technology into the actual setting where work is done. Business decisions, operational approvals, and confirmation of results still belong to the people within the enterprise who have the corresponding responsibilities. Chapter 2 explains the origins of this role; Chapter 3 distinguishes it from software agents and digital roles.

The book has five parts and eighteen chapters. They proceed from understanding the problem to deciding on investment, completing delivery, judging business outcomes, and building an organization's ability to keep putting AI to work. On a first reading, follow the chapters in order. If you have a specific question, start with the relevant chapter in the contents.

A judgment runs throughout the book: whenever you increase investment or expand use, make clear what work is to be completed, what evidence supports the decision, and who will receive and accept the result. When conditions change, the original decision must be open to review and revision. Chapter 1 introduces the problem; Chapter 18 applies this approach to the schedule and decision record for a first project.

If you are preparing your first project, you can start with Chapters 1 and 18 and then use the table below. Six steps lead to ten main forms. Fill in what the current decision requires first; for detailed records, you may refer to materials your enterprise already has.

| Decision to make now | Chapters to consult | Worksheets to use first |
|---|---|---|
| Which task to choose and how much investment to permit | Chapters 4–5 | 04-1 Task selection and interviews; 05-1 Investment, baseline, and conditions for continuing |
| Who approves, carries out, and accepts the work | Chapter 6 | 06-1 Responsibilities and takeover |
| How the actual work is completed | Chapter 8 | 08-1 Field observation and workflow replay |
| What the system may do and what evidence permits release | Chapters 9–10 | 09-1 Information governance; 09-2 Action governance; 10-1 Production admission |
| Whether employees use it and whether the results hold up | Chapters 11–12 | 11-1 Adoption records; 12-1 Business outcome verification |
| Whether to continue, adjust, or end the next stage | Chapter 18 | 18-1 Decision on the next stage |

Read Chapter 7 as well when buying external services. Turn to Chapter 13 when a project fails or you are considering an exit. Chapters 14–17 address reuse of deliverables, service revenue and costs, delivery across several parties, and maintenance of experience-based rules. Return to Chapters 2–3 when identifying what you are recruiting or procuring. The project, staffing, and renewal accounts in Chapter 15 mainly help people running a delivery business recalculate the economics. Buyers can focus on the scope of charges, continuing support, and the burden of replacing a provider.

When reading the cases, pay attention to the task, period, and conditions to which each number refers. Real cases, illustrative calculations, and the author's analytical frameworks are labeled. Planned targets, demonstration performance, and actual operating results are different kinds of evidence. The sections labeled “Implementation considerations” and “Business and finance” add delivery detail and business judgment, respectively.

Similar teaching scenarios do not necessarily share a single set of figures. Chapters 4–10, for example, repeatedly use a settlement task to illustrate task selection, budgeting, contracts, observation, design, and production admission. The cash budget of 66,000 renminbi (RMB) in Chapter 5 and the RMB 100,000 service package in Chapter 7 are independent assumptions and must not be added together. The monthly outcome account in Chapter 12 does not continue either of those investment accounts. The repair intake in Chapter 14, inspection in Chapter 16, payment exceptions in Chapter 17, and spare-parts inquiries in Chapter 18 each serve a different purpose; read them within the records of their respective chapters. In a real project, use the same task identifier to connect your own records.

Eighteen worksheets appear at the back of the book for use with the chapters. Before filling them in, define the task, the responsible person, and the scope of observation. Where evidence is not yet available, leave a field blank or write “unknown,” then arrange to obtain the missing record. The worksheets help a team reach a shared judgment; they do not replace execution and verification.

To trace the evidence, follow the numbered citations in the text to “Notes and references” at the back of the book. For example, [1-1] means the first note in Chapter 1. Abbreviated note numbers inside figures also refer to that chapter. In the electronic edition, you can click the citation numbers and the source links in the notes to find the original material and the explanation of how it is used.
